A mechanical watch has no battery and no chip. It runs on a wound spring, and it stays accurate because of one small part that keeps interrupting that spring.

## The spring

Winding the crown tightens a coiled strip of metal called the mainspring. Left alone, it would unwind in a few seconds and the hands would spin wildly. A full wind usually holds about 40 hours of running time, so something has to let the energy out slowly.

## The part that says stop

That job belongs to the escapement. It sits at the end of the gear train and does two things:

- It locks the gears, then lets them move one tooth at a time.
- Each time it lets go, it gives a small push to the balance wheel.

The balance wheel swings back and forth on a hair-thin spring, like a pendulum that works in any position. In most modern watches it swings **28,800 times an hour**, which is eight ticks every second. The ticking you hear is the escapement locking and unlocking.

## How good is it

A watch certified as a chronometer has to stay within about four seconds slow and six seconds fast per day. A cheap quartz watch beats that easily, because its crystal vibrates 32,768 times a second and barely drifts.

So a mechanical watch is the worse clock. People still buy them because you can open the back and see all of it working.
